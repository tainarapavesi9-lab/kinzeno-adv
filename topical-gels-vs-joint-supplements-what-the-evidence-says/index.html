Topical Gels vs Joint Supplements: What the Evidence Says

By the editorial team. Updated 9 October 2026.

See a GP or physio. Persistent, worsening or unexplained joint pain should be assessed by a GP or physiotherapist. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Chemist shelves and online ads offer a long list of options for sore joints: anti-inflammatory gels, herbal creams, massage gels, glucosamine tablets, turmeric capsules and more. This guide summarises what Australian guidance and published reviews say about each group. The short version is that the evidence is stronger for exercise than for any gel or pill, and that the products differ in how they are regulated.

Group 1: anti-inflammatory gels sold as medicines

Topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) gels, such as diclofenac gels, are medicines that are assessed by the TGA and carry an ARTG number. The RACGP knee and hip osteoarthritis guideline (2018, under revision) does not make a firm recommendation for or against topical NSAIDs, and says a short trial, stopped if there is no benefit, can be reasonable. Side effects, especially skin reactions, should be watched for. Ask a pharmacist whether one is suitable for you.

Group 2: herbal and botanical gels

A Cochrane review of topical herbal treatments for osteoarthritis found only a few small trials. In one trial of 174 people with hand osteoarthritis, arnica gel appeared to work about as well as an ibuprofen gel; in another, comfrey extract gel probably improved pain; and capsicum gel probably did not. The review called for larger studies, and it was published in 2013. A newer small trial of a curcumin gel in knee osteoarthritis has also been reported, but it is a single study of 60 people and needs confirmation.

For blends of botanicals sold as massage products, such as Kinzeno, we found no controlled trials at all.

Group 3: glucosamine and chondroitin

The RACGP guideline suggests not offering glucosamine, chondroitin, or the combination for knee and hip osteoarthritis, citing low to very low quality evidence. Some people say they feel better on them, and a number of trials are funded by supplement companies. If you are taking them, speak to your pharmacist before starting or stopping, especially if you use blood-thinning medicines.

Group 4: turmeric (curcumin) and boswellia

Oral turmeric and boswellia have been studied in several small trials and meta-analyses, with some reports of modest pain reduction in osteoarthritis. Study quality varies, and absorption depends on the formulation. These are oral supplements, which is not the same as a gel.

Why regulation matters

Medicines on the ARTG must meet standards for quality and, for many, evidence. Cosmetics and massage products are not allowed to claim to treat or relieve a condition. If a product page promises relief from arthritis, either the product should be on the ARTG, or the claim is a problem. The TGA's Therapeutic Goods Advertising Code also limits testimonials. A sensible order of priorities

  1. Get a diagnosis: see a GP or physio.
  2. Start with exercise, weight management and pacing.
  3. Discuss medicines, including medicinal gels, with your GP or pharmacist.
  4. If you want to try a massage gel for comfort, treat it as a comfort product, use it as the label says (how to use a topical gel), and expect no medical effect.
  5. Be sceptical of supplements promoted with testimonials or urgency.

How to read a study before you trust a claim

Look for five things. First, was there a comparison group that received a dummy treatment? Second, how many people took part, and for how long? Third, who paid for the study? Fourth, was the result about pain you can feel, or only a lab measure? Fifth, has the finding been repeated by other teams? A single small trial funded by the company that sells the product is a starting point, not proof.

Also remember that in joint pain trials, people given a dummy treatment often improve noticeably, partly because pain fluctuates and partly because expectation matters. That is why a testimonial from a friend or an online reviewer, however sincere, is weak evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Do topical joint gels work?

Medicinal NSAID gels have some evidence for short-term pain in some people, though the RACGP does not make a firm recommendation. For botanical massage gels, evidence is limited or absent.

Does glucosamine work for knee osteoarthritis?

The RACGP guideline suggests not offering it, based on low to very low quality evidence.

Does turmeric help joint pain?

Some small trials of oral turmeric report modest benefits, but the quality is mixed. Ask a pharmacist before starting, especially if you take other medicines.

What is the difference between a massage gel and a medicine?

A medicine on the ARTG has been assessed by the TGA and can make approved claims. A massage or cosmetic product cannot claim to treat or relieve a condition.
