Joint Pain Over 50: Causes, Red Flags and What Helps

By the editorial team. Updated 9 October 2026.

See a GP or physio. Persistent, worsening or unexplained joint pain should be assessed by a GP or physiotherapist. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Aching knees, stiff hands and sore hips become more common after 50, and for most people the cause is something that can be assessed and managed. This guide summarises common causes, the warning signs that need prompt attention, and the approaches with the best evidence, drawing on Australian sources such as Healthdirect and the RACGP guideline for knee and hip osteoarthritis. It is general information, not a diagnosis.

Common causes of joint pain in later life

  • Osteoarthritis is the most common cause. It often affects the knees, hips, hands and spine, and tends to cause pain with activity and stiffness that eases within about half an hour of getting moving.
  • Inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis, typically causes longer morning stiffness, swelling and tiredness, and often affects joints on both sides of the body.
  • Gout causes sudden, intense pain, redness and swelling, often at the base of the big toe.
  • Tendon and bursa problems cause pain around a joint rather than inside it, and often follow a change in activity.
  • Injury, including meniscal tears in the knee, or pain referred from the spine or hip.

Telling these apart matters because the treatments differ. That is why an assessment is worthwhile.

Red flags: see a doctor promptly

  • A joint that is suddenly hot, red and swollen, especially with a fever.
  • Pain following a fall or injury, or being unable to put weight on the leg.
  • Night pain that wakes you, or pain with unexplained weight loss.
  • Morning stiffness lasting more than about an hour, or several joints swelling at once.
  • Numbness, weakness or a joint that locks or gives way.

Healthdirect advises seeing your GP for joint pain, sooner if you have trouble walking, reduced movement, or grinding or clicking, or if it affects daily life. Call 000 for emergencies. The Healthdirect line on 1800 022 222 offers advice from a nurse at any hour.

What the evidence supports first

For knee and hip osteoarthritis, Australian guidance puts three things first: education about the condition, exercise, and weight management if relevant. Exercise is the standout: guided exercise strengthens the muscles around a joint and has been shown to reduce pain and improve function. A physiotherapist can build a plan around your joint and fitness. Programs such as GLA:D Australia combine education with neuromuscular exercise for hip and knee osteoarthritis and are delivered by trained clinicians. Weight loss reduces load on the knees and hips, and even modest changes can help.

Other sensible steps include supportive footwear, pacing your activity, walking aids if recommended, and using heat or cold if they feel helpful.

Where medicines, gels and supplements fit

Pain relief medicines, including some anti-inflammatory gels sold in pharmacies, are options to discuss with your GP or pharmacist. The RACGP guideline does not make a firm recommendation for or against topical anti-inflammatory gels, and says that a short trial that is stopped if it does not help can be reasonable. For glucosamine and chondroitin supplements, it suggests not offering them. We compare these in topical gels vs joint supplements.

Walking the dog and daily life with sore knees

Many people with knee pain want to keep walking, and staying active is usually encouraged. Try shorter, more frequent walks on flat ground, warm up with gentle knee bends first, wear supportive shoes and use a lead that does not pull you off balance. If walking reliably aggravates the pain or you start to limp, ask a physio for a tailored plan rather than stopping altogether.

Questions to take to your GP or physio

  • What do you think is causing my pain, and do I need a scan or blood test?
  • Which exercises are best for my joint, and how often should I do them?
  • Could my weight, footwear or work be contributing?
  • Which pain relief medicines are suitable given my other health conditions?
  • Is there a local program, such as a supervised group exercise class?

Writing down when the pain occurs, how long morning stiffness lasts, and what makes it better or worse will make the appointment more productive.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common cause of knee pain over 50?

Osteoarthritis is the most common cause, but tendon problems, injuries and inflammatory conditions are also possible, so persistent pain should be assessed.

Is morning stiffness normal?

Mild stiffness that eases within about 30 minutes is typical of osteoarthritis. Stiffness lasting an hour or more, or with swelling in several joints, should be checked by a GP.

When should I see a GP about joint pain?

Healthdirect advises seeing a GP for joint pain, and sooner if it affects walking or daily life. Seek urgent care for a hot, red, swollen joint with fever, or pain after an injury.

What helps knee osteoarthritis the most?

Exercise, education and weight management have the strongest support. A physiotherapist can design a plan for you.
