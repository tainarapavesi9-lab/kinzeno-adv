Kinzeno Side Effects and Who Should Avoid It

By the editorial team. Updated 9 October 2026.

See a GP or physio. Persistent, worsening or unexplained joint pain should be assessed by a GP or physiotherapist. This page is general information, not medical advice.

We found no clinical safety data on the finished Kinzeno product, so this guide is based on its listed ingredients, the seller's own safety notes and general advice about topical products. It is not a substitute for advice from a pharmacist or your GP.

What the seller says about safety

According to the seller, Kinzeno is for external use only. Its FAQ advises avoiding contact with eyes and mucous membranes, washing hands after applying, stopping use if irritation occurs and consulting a health professional before use if you have medical conditions. It also states the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.

Possible reactions to look for

  • Redness, itching, stinging or a rash where the gel was applied. Botanical ingredients such as arnica and boswellia can trigger contact dermatitis in some people.
  • Dryness or peeling with frequent use.
  • Allergic reactions. People allergic to daisy-family plants (ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold, chamomile) may react to arnica.
  • Eye irritation if the gel is transferred from the hands.

Cooling sensations are expected with some gels. Burning, blistering or a spreading rash is not, and is a reason to stop.

Who should check first

  • Anyone with eczema, psoriasis or very sensitive skin in the area.
  • People who are pregnant or breastfeeding. We found no safety data on this product in pregnancy.
  • People taking blood thinners or other regular medicines, because some herbal products can interact with them, and because bruising or swelling needs medical review.
  • Children. The product is aimed at adults.
  • Anyone with a known plant or fragrance allergy.

A pharmacist can check an ingredient list against your medicines in a couple of minutes, and it costs nothing.

A simple patch test

Put a small amount on a coin-sized patch of inner forearm, leave it for 24 hours and watch for redness or itching. If you see a reaction, do not use it on your joints. Never apply to broken, burnt or sunburnt skin, and do not cover treated skin with tight bandages or heat packs unless a health professional suggests it.

When to stop and who to call

  • Stop using the gel and wash the area with water if irritation develops.
  • See your GP if a rash does not settle in a few days.
  • Call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 if the gel is swallowed or gets in the eyes and you are unsure what to do.
  • Call 000 for swelling of the face or lips, trouble breathing or a widespread rash.

Pain that is worsening, a joint that is hot, red and swollen, fever, or pain after an injury needs medical attention rather than a gel. See joint pain over 50 for red flags.

How reactions are usually handled

Most reactions to topical products are mild and settle within a few days once the product is stopped. Rinse the area with plain water, avoid scratching, and use a simple, fragrance-free moisturiser if the skin feels dry. A pharmacist can suggest an over-the-counter option for mild irritation. If you are unsure whether a rash is related to a product, take the pack with you when you see your GP or pharmacist so they can check the ingredient list.

If you do react, it is worth reporting it. In Australia, adverse reactions to cosmetic products can be raised with the seller and, if serious, with the ACCC. Reactions to therapeutic goods can be reported to the TGA. Keeping the batch number and a photo of the reaction makes any report more useful.

It is also worth remembering that a pleasant cooling feel is not a safety signal. Some irritants feel cool or tingly at first. Judge a product by how your skin looks and feels over the following day, not just by the first few minutes.
