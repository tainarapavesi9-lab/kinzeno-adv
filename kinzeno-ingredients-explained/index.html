Kinzeno Ingredients Explained: MSM, Arnica, Boswellia and More

By the editorial team. Updated 9 October 2026.

See a GP or physio. Persistent, worsening or unexplained joint pain should be assessed by a GP or physiotherapist. This page is general information, not medical advice.

According to the seller, Kinzeno contains magnesium (sulfate and chloride), MSM (dimethyl sulfone), Arnica montana, Boswellia carterii (frankincense), vitamin B complex and horse chestnut (aescin), plus around ten other ingredients such as vitamin E and vitamin B5. The seller does not publish amounts. This guide explains each ingredient and what the research says when it is used on the skin.

Before the list: what we could not check

We did not find a complete ingredient list with amounts, and ingredient lists on third-party pages differ from each other, so the label on the pack you receive is the reference. Australian rules require cosmetic products to carry an ingredient list, so you can compare the label with the website. We also found no published trials of this finished formula. What follows is about the individual ingredients, which is not the same as evidence for the product.

Magnesium (sulfate and chloride)

Magnesium is a mineral your body needs, and magnesium salts are common in bath products and body gels. Studies of whether magnesium applied to the skin raises levels in the body are limited and mixed, so claims that a gel "delivers" magnesium to a joint should be treated carefully. In a massage product, magnesium salts may also simply affect texture and skin feel.

MSM (dimethyl sulfone)

MSM is a sulfur compound found in small amounts in some foods and widely sold as a supplement. Most of the human research we are aware of looks at oral MSM in small trials of knee osteoarthritis, with mixed quality. We did not find robust trials of MSM applied as a gel, so for a topical product this remains an open question.

Arnica (Arnica montana)

Arnica is a mountain plant traditionally used in creams for bruises and sore muscles. A 2013 Cochrane review of topical herbal treatments for osteoarthritis included one trial of 174 people with hand osteoarthritis in which arnica gel appeared to perform similarly to an ibuprofen gel. That is one trial, in the hands rather than the knees, so it is not strong evidence. Arnica can also cause contact dermatitis, especially in people allergic to plants in the daisy family, and it should not be put on broken skin.

Boswellia (frankincense)

Boswellia resin has a long history in traditional medicine. A meta-analysis of trials of oral boswellia in osteoarthritis suggested possible benefits in pain and function, though the trials were small. We did not find trials of boswellia applied to the skin for joint pain, so oral results cannot be assumed to apply to a gel.

Vitamin B complex, vitamin E and horse chestnut (aescin)

B vitamins and vitamin E are common in skin products as conditioning ingredients. Horse chestnut extract, rich in aescin, is traditionally used in products for tired legs and vein health. None of these is established as a treatment for joint pain when applied to the skin. As with any botanical extract, a minority of people react to it.

What this means in practice

Putting familiar-sounding ingredients in one gel does not show that the gel works for joint pain. A cooling gel and a few minutes of massage can feel pleasant, and that may be a perfectly good reason to use one. For the approaches with stronger evidence, see joint pain over 50: causes and what helps. To understand possible reactions, read side effects and who should avoid it.

Questions to ask about any botanical gel

  • Is the full ingredient list, with the order of ingredients, printed on the pack?
  • Are there fragrances or essential oils that could irritate sensitive skin?
  • Does the seller cite published trials of the finished product, or only of single ingredients?
  • Does the product claim to treat a condition? If so, it should be on the ARTG.
  • Do I take medicines that could interact with a herbal ingredient?

These five questions will help you assess not just Kinzeno but almost any herbal gel or cream sold online.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main ingredients in Kinzeno?

According to the seller: magnesium sulfate and chloride, MSM, arnica, boswellia, vitamin B complex and horse chestnut (aescin), plus other ingredients.

Does the seller list the amounts of each ingredient?

Not in the pages we read, so the dose of each ingredient is unknown.

Is there research on topical MSM or boswellia for joint pain?

We did not find robust trials of either applied to the skin. Studies on boswellia mostly look at oral forms.

Is arnica safe on the skin?

Many people use arnica gels without trouble, but it can cause skin reactions, particularly in people allergic to daisy-family plants, and it should not be used on broken skin.
